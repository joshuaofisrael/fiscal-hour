FDIC insurance and SIPC protection are both safety nets for money held at financial firms, but they cover different things. The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation insures deposits at insured banks if the bank fails. The Securities Investor Protection Corporation protects customers of a failed member brokerage firm whose cash and securities are missing. Neither one protects against a fall in the value of an investment.

## What FDIC insurance covers

Checked on 21 November 2026, the FDIC’s [understanding-deposit-insurance page](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance/understanding-deposit-insurance) still states two phrasings of the same limit. The opening says the FDIC insures deposits to at least $250,000 per depositor, per ownership category, at each insured bank. The limits section says coverage is $250,000 per depositor, per FDIC-insured bank, for each account ownership category. The page says it was last updated on 1 April 2024. The dollar figure on the page had not changed when this article was rechecked. The FDIC also says that since it was founded in 1933, no depositor has lost a penny of insured funds. That sentence is the agency’s.

The page says the insurance covers checking accounts, savings accounts, money market deposit accounts, certificates of deposit, and cashier’s checks, money orders and other official items issued by a bank. It says the FDIC does not cover stocks, bonds, mutual funds, annuities, life insurance, municipal securities, crypto assets, Treasury bills, bonds or notes, or the contents of a safe deposit box, even when they are bought through a bank. Treasury securities are on that not-covered list because they are not deposits. How the ownership categories add up is in the [FDIC limit explainer](/articles/2026-10-29-fdic-deposit-insurance-limit/).

FDIC coverage is automatic when you open a deposit account at an insured bank. You do not apply for it.

## What SIPC protection covers

SIPC is not a government agency and it is not insurance in the same sense. [Investor.gov](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/securities-investor-protection-corporation-sipc), checked on 21 November 2026, calls SIPC a non-government entity. The page says protection can apply when a member brokerage goes out of business and cannot meet its obligations to customers, up to $500,000 per customer, including a $250,000 limit for cash. It says SIPC protects most types of securities, such as stocks, bonds and mutual funds. It also says SIPC does not protect against a decline in market value, does not cover investment contracts that are not registered with the SEC, and does not apply when cash or securities are placed with a firm that is not a SIPC member.

SIPC restores what should have been in the account, such as shares the firm held for the customer. It does not make up losses because a stock price fell.

## Side by side

- **Who backs it:** FDIC is an independent federal agency. SIPC is a nonprofit membership corporation created by federal law.
- **What triggers it:** a bank failure for the FDIC, a brokerage firm failure with missing customer assets for SIPC.
- **Limits:** $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category for the FDIC. $500,000 per customer, including $250,000 cash, for SIPC.
- **Market losses:** not covered by either.

## Where people get confused

Cash in a brokerage account is often "swept" into deposit accounts at partner banks. In that case the cash may be FDIC insured at those banks rather than SIPC protected, and the limits work differently. Firms describe their sweep programs in account disclosures, and that is the document to read.

Crypto platforms and payment apps are another source of confusion. Holding crypto at a platform does not make it FDIC insured. The FDIC page lists crypto assets among the things deposit insurance does not cover. To check a bank, the FDIC points readers to its BankFind Suite. To check a brokerage, Investor.gov says to use SIPC’s membership lookup, and that firms have to say if they are not members.

A fund’s annual cost is a different question from either protection scheme. That definition is in [what an expense ratio measures](/articles/2026-10-31-what-a-fund-expense-ratio-is/). Series I savings bonds are Treasury securities, and the FDIC page lists Treasury bills, bonds and notes as outside deposit insurance. How the bond’s interest is set is in [how I bond interest works](/articles/2026-10-30-how-i-bond-interest-works/).
