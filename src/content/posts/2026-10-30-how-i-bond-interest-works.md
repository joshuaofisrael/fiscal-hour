A Series I savings bond earns a rate that changes every six months, based on inflation, according to the Treasury's [I bonds interest rates](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/savings-bonds/i-bonds/i-bonds-interest-rates/) page on TreasuryDirect. The page says the rate can go up and can go down, and that an I bond earns interest until it is cashed or reaches 30 years old, whichever comes first.

The Treasury says the interest on an I bond combines two parts: a fixed rate and an inflation rate. The fixed rate is known when the bond is bought and never changes for the life of that bond. The Treasury announces a fixed rate every May 1 and Nov. 1, and that rate applies to all I bonds issued during the following six months. The inflation rate is also set every May 1 and Nov. 1, and the page says it is based on changes in the non-seasonally adjusted Consumer Price Index for all Urban Consumers, CPI-U, for all items including food and energy. Unlike the fixed rate, the inflation rate applies to every I bond ever issued, for six months at a time.

The combined rate, which the Treasury also calls the composite rate or earnings rate, comes from a published formula: the fixed rate, plus two times the semiannual inflation rate, plus the fixed rate times the semiannual inflation rate. Checked on 20 November 2026, the [rates page](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/savings-bonds/i-bonds/i-bonds-interest-rates/) works the current case for bonds issued from 1 November 2026 through 30 April 2027. The fixed rate in that example is 1.40%, and the semiannual inflation rate is 2.04%. The page adds 0.0140 + (2 × 0.0204) + (0.0140 × 0.0204), which is 0.0140 + 0.0408 + 0.0002856, or 0.0550856, and rounds that to 5.51%.

That 5.51% is the composite rate for new issues in this six-month window. It is not the rate on every bond already outstanding. The fixed rate stays with the bond that received it. The same table lists a 5.00% composite, for the earning period that starts in November 2026, on bonds bought from May 2026 through October 2026. When this page was first published, the worked example on the site was the prior new-issue rate, 4.26%, from a 0.90% fixed rate and a 1.67% semiannual inflation rate for bonds issued 1 May 2026 through 31 October 2026. The inflation piece of the formula uses the non-seasonally adjusted CPI-U. How that index differs from the PCE price index the Fed targets is a separate question, covered in [CPI vs PCE](/articles/2026-11-11-cpi-vs-pce-inflation/). The latest CPI print is the [October report](/articles/2026-11-11-october-cpi-0-4-percent-core-holds-0-3/). The next CPI date is on the [release calendar](/calendar/).

The Treasury says deflation can pull the combined rate below the fixed rate, as long as the fixed rate is not zero. If the inflation component is negative enough to push the combined rate below zero, the page says the Treasury stops at zero. Its history tables show negative semiannual inflation rates were set on May 1, 2009, at -2.78%, and on May 1, 2015, at -0.80%.

Although new rates are announced in May and November, the date a new rate starts for a given bond depends on its issue month. The page says the rate changes every six months from the issue date, so a bond issued in January moves to a new rate on July 1 and Jan. 1, and a bond issued in May moves on Nov. 1 and May 1.

I bonds earn interest from the first day of the month they are bought, the Treasury says. Twice a year, all the interest earned in the previous six months is added to the bond's principal value, and the next rate applies to that larger amount. The page calls this semiannual compounding. Investor.gov, run by the Securities and Exchange Commission, defines [compound interest](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/compound-interest) as interest paid on principal and on accumulated interest.

One more rule affects the value shown on a bond. The Treasury says that if a bond is cashed before it is five years old, the last three months of interest are not paid, so values shown in TreasuryDirect and in its Savings Bond Calculator for bonds under five years old leave those three months out. An I bond is a Treasury security, which is a different promise from a bank deposit. The [FDIC insurance limit](/articles/2026-10-29-fdic-deposit-insurance-limit/) applies to deposits at insured banks, and the FDIC’s own list of what it does not insure includes Treasury bills, notes and bonds. This article describes the Treasury’s published rules. It does not say whether anyone should buy, hold or cash an I bond.
