Wall Street's hottest lending business has found a big target in the Gulf, and a stubborn one. "Private Credit's $100 Billion Saudi Prize Is Proving Hard to Win," [Bloomberg posted on X](https://x.com/business/status/2124478029397102643) on Sunday.

## The pitch

Private credit funds lend directly to companies instead of through banks or bond markets. According to the [Bloomberg report carried by Livemint](https://www.livemint.com/market/private-credit-s-100-billion-saudi-prize-is-proving-hard-to-win-11795348703055.html), many of these firms believe Saudi Arabia offers an estimated $100 billion opportunity as Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman pushes to diversify the economy away from oil.

The returns look tempting. Dino Kronfol, Franklin Templeton's head of global sukuk and MENA fixed income, told Bloomberg they could reach 16% or more, roughly twice the levels in more established markets, reflecting extra risk and smaller funds.

## The problem

Getting the money out of the door is slow. Bloomberg describes a recent $150 million financing for a little-known Saudi firm, backed by Goldman Sachs, HSBC, Fortress Investment Group and at least three regional managers, that took months to complete. People familiar with it said a deal like that would take weeks in many other markets.

Fund managers cite regulatory uncertainty, licensing that can take up to a year and rules on local presence. Deals are also small. Bloomberg says many come in below $50 million, and Benefit Street Partners, a Franklin Templeton firm with about $97 billion under management, estimates that about 60% of regional private credit deals are below $30 million.

"I don't think people are running away but it takes a certain kind of institution to make the jump," said Berkay Oncel of SC Lowy.

## Who is still pushing ahead

Some managers are committed. Janus Henderson plans a final close on a $300 million fund early next year and is looking at about a dozen opportunities, many in Saudi Arabia, according to its head of MENA private credit, Sikander Ahmed. Bloomberg says partnerships between international managers and local funds are one way to bridge the gap.

"Each deal that closes makes the next one easier to assess," said Kurt Davis Jr. of Alvarez & Marsal.

## Why it matters for investors

Private credit has grown fast in the US and Europe and is looking for new markets. If the Gulf is slower to crack than hoped, more money stays at home, where competition is already squeezing returns. For readers holding private credit funds or the listed managers behind them, the Saudi story is a reminder that headline-sized opportunities do not always turn into deals quickly.
